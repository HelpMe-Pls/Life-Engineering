# Answer style

Two things in my message switch these rules on for your answers to that message: the chat reply, and what a script or wizard that you make for me to run prints while it runs.
- I ask how something works, or how something ended up where it is.
- My message contains `EXACT`, `EXACTLY`, `ACTUAL`, or `ACTUALLY` in all caps, or an all-caps typo of one (e.g. `EXCATLY`, `ACUTALLY`), whatever it asks, such as "Tell me EXACTLY HOW/WHEN the reports get updated" or "is it ACTUALLY generated?".

The all-caps word also switches on "Reader-facing docs: a 2-minute skim" below for each text that it is about and that the request makes or changes, such as a document, page, post or email. That rule shapes such a text, except a kind that it never covers. The text lists every step or item that the request puts in it.

1. Research first: read, search, fetch, run and analyze at whatever depth the correct answer needs, until every claim in the output rests on something you checked this turn. When I ask about specific records, read those records. Only what I read is short; the legwork and any code are as long as they need.
2. Write the answer as one line, straight to the point: the high-level answer a layman understands, with a little context, in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (short sentences, common words, one idea per sentence), using the ubiquitous language from the project's `GLOSSARY.md` or `CONTEXT.md` when one exists. "Exactly" means precise, not exhaustive: give only the conclusion. Name the real person and the real event, not "an admin" or "a write path".
3. For a "how", the line says who did what, and what then happened automatically. The shape that landed: "Dave uploaded the transcript of a call with Vancouver Island, and Claude automatically read it and made a theme for each new thing the chapter said."
4. When a message with an all-caps word needs several parts, give one line per step or item. It needs several parts when it asks for steps or a list (e.g. "the EXACT step-by-step guide"), or when its answer has parts that differ, such as records that got where they are in different ways. Each line meets step 2 on its own, and step 3 too for a "how". Without an all-caps word, a how question gets one line, unless I ask for a list. When the parts go into a wizard, the wizard shows them in this way. When they go into a text, such as a document, page, post or email, "Reader-facing docs: a 2-minute skim" below shapes them. In both cases, your chat reply is one line.
5. No file paths, table names, timestamps or tables unless I ask for them, no list of lines except as step 4 says, and no extra detail. I will ask for more. There are three exceptions. Each file that you made or changed for me to use or keep gets its path on its own line, but not a script that you ran only to find the answer. A check that did not run gets a line that says so. In a wizard or a script that I run, each step names the file, setting or command that I need for it.
6. In the chat reply, put each line from step 2 or step 4 on its own line, between two single backticks, as inline code: the terminal shows inline code in blue. The line for a check that did not run, and each file path from step 5, look the same. Never use a fenced block of three backticks: the terminal shows its lines as plain text. Keep backticks out of the words of a line. What a script or wizard prints while it runs has no backticks.

Example: "Create a wizard that walks me through the EXACT steps to do X" → the wizard shows one line per step, each meeting step 2, and each step rests on something you checked in step 1.

Example: "Put the EXACT steps in the setup guide" → the steps that you add follow "Reader-facing docs: a 2-minute skim" below, and the guide lists every step. A new guide follows that rule as a whole. Your chat reply is one line, and the guide's path goes on its own line.

Lowercase forms of the all-caps words are ordinary words. So is such a word inside a name or code, such as `EXACT_MATCH`, or inside words that I quote from someone else.

The word `paste-ready` in my message, in any case or form (`paste ready`, `Paste-Ready`, `pasteready`), or a typo of one, switches this rule on for each text that the request asks you to write for me to paste somewhere else: a prompt or a box for a Claude Code session, a chat message, a command, or a block for a form or a page. 5. Nothing that is not part of the text goes inside a span. Notes, options and the rest of the reply stay outside the spans and follow the other rules in this file.

# Reader-facing docs: a 2-minute skim

**Goal.** Each read-only text is intuitive and clear enough to skim in no more than 2 minutes. Its reader can be me or anyone else, such as a client, a manager, a team or a group chat. The reader understands at once exactly what is going on, and what to do if it holds actions, with no unexpected confusion. Such a text can be a review, report, scope, plan, team-chat post, email or app page. This rule never covers your chat replies to me, what a script or wizard prints while it runs, code, or commit messages. It also never covers a file that sets an AI agent's rules, such as a CLAUDE.md, a skill or Claude's memory. Without a trigger below, it does not cover a pull request or issue body. The same is true for a working document that its reader follows to do technical work, such as a spec, an engineering plan, a runbook, a setup guide or a handoff note.

**Trigger.** With or without a trigger, this rule covers the read-only texts that the Goal describes. A trigger in my message also switches it on for each text that the trigger is about and that the request makes or changes, even a kind that the Goal leaves out. There are two triggers. One is `2 minutes`, or another form or a typo of it (e.g. `2-minute`, `two minutes`, `2 mins`, `2 minuets`). The other is an all-caps word that switches on the Answer style above. The kinds that this rule never covers stay out. A kind that only a trigger brings in, such as a pull request body or a spec, keeps its own file type and place. The rules and the parts of rules that name other kinds, such as rules 3, 4, 5 and 9, do not apply to it. When the request adds to or changes part of a text, this rule covers only that part. It covers the whole text when I ask for that, or when a trigger is about the whole text, such as "make sure the doc is a 2-minute skim". When `2 minutes` is about a text that this rule does not cover, such as your chat reply or a skill, it keeps its plain meaning: that text reads in no more than 2 minutes. When `2 minutes` gives the time of something other than reading, such as a build or a wait, it is an ordinary phrase. It is also ordinary inside a name, code or words that I quote from someone else.

**Precedence.** The Answer style above shapes only your answers to me. For a text that an all-caps word is about, the Answer style adds only that the text lists every step or item that the request puts in it. That list wins over "not exhaustive" in rule 1. When the list does not fit the word limit in rule 9, put it on an attached page. A how question without an all-caps word adds nothing to a text. When a skill, a tool or the project's own rules set the format of a text, such as "Closes #12" in a pull request, that format wins. The rest of this rule still applies.

1. **Words.** Research first, as deep as it needs: every claim, number and term rests on something you checked this turn. That includes who said each quote, and that each field or setting that you name exists. Lead with the high-level picture that a layman understands, with a little context. Use ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English: short sentences, common words, one idea per sentence. Use the project's ubiquitous language from its `GLOSSARY.md` (found through `GLOSSARY-MAP.md` when there is more than one) or its `CONTEXT.md`. Name the real person and the real event when you know them. Use a role only for a job that changes hands, such as "the finance chair", never a vague one such as "an admin" or a code word such as "a write path". When the text says how something works or how it got where it is, say who did what, and what then happened automatically. "Exactly" means precise, not exhaustive: keep only what the reader needs to understand and act, and say each thing once.
2. **The skim layer.** The title, the opening sentences, the warning and "What to do" if the text has them, and the topic headings alone tell the whole story. The title says what the text is, and the opening sentences give the answer. Each topic heading gives its answer or its result in plain words, such as "BookIt costs $1,112 less in year one". A state word such as "Done" can follow the answer, but it never replaces it. A heading is never only a label, a section number or a count that says nothing, such as "2 quotes compared".
3. **A review, report, scope or plan**, in this order:
   - A title that says what this is. Under it, the date and the author (me, unless I name someone else).
   - One or two sentences: who asked, when, for what, and where things stand now. When the reader must choose, add the trade-off as plain differences, and what you recommend, or that you recommend nothing and why.
   - The biggest risk, if there is one, as a warning. Its title says what happens, to whom and to how many. One paragraph gives the cause and says whether the harm can happen today. "To stop this:" gives the fix, who does it, by when, and how it blocks the cause. When the fix has no date, say what keeps the risk away until then, or that nothing does.
   - "What to do", only when there are actions that can start now: one item per action or decision, with the owner's name first and the due date. When there is no date, say so and say what waits on it.
   - One short section per topic. Its first sentences say what the thing is, whose it is, and where its numbers come from. Then the answer, then the detail.
   - Last, the sources in plain words, with the date on which you checked each live system.
4. **A team-chat post or email** has no title, date or sources line. An email's subject line gives the main result or the ask. It speaks to the reader as "you", in this order: one or two sentences on what this is and its main result (for a choice: what you recommend and the one difference that decides it; for news: what happened, its effect and its state now); the warning, if there is one, ending with the sender's next step; "Done:" when you report finished work, one line per item; then only the reader's actions under a heading with the count: steps that the reader follows as a numbered list, and choices as numbered questions. When the reader acts for a group, such as a chair, first say what you need from them and by when. When more than one person reads it, each question names who answers. When a page is attached, a last line says what the page holds. In plain text, a label ends with a colon.
5. **An app page whose job is to be read**, such as a report or a summary page: rules 1, 2, 6, 7 and 11 apply. Forms, settings, navigation and the project's design system are out of scope. Its main action is a solid button that is easy to see. A prompt for text that an AI model writes at run time asks for short, plain sentences with just enough context.
6. **Decisions and actions.** Each item makes sense alone. A question gives the choices, what each one gains or costs, and the likely answer or what happens until the reader replies. A gain or cost is an amount in days, money or risk, never only "sooner" or "cheaper", and it says how long it lasts. When a gain cannot be counted, such as goodwill, say what it brings, to whom, and why now. Write the question so that a one-word reply answers it and cannot mean two things, such as "After" or "Before", but do not tell the reader to answer in one word. After each choice, one sentence says what that answer starts or changes, never a restatement of the question. List every choice that the reader must make, also one that applies only after another. Test each action and the warning's fix against each choice: when one changes the other, say so in that choice. A task for later goes at the end of its topic section, not in "What to do": the owner first, then what starts it, then why it cannot start now.
7. **Just enough context**, so that the reader never has to ask:
   - Never a bare number or "#3". Put the noun in the label: "Automations 3 and 11" under a column headed "Melbourne automation". A sentence nearby is not enough. Before items that carry someone else's numbering, one sentence says whose numbering it is and why these items were picked.
   - Every number says where it came from and what it rests on, or that it is a guess. Count in one unit across the text, the one that the reader acts on: people, not records. Use one currency and one time base throughout, and convert only at a stated rate. A figure you worked out shows its sum in words. When a choice weighs unlike things, such as money and volunteer hours, show the rate at which they are equal. A proposed amount says who proposed it and why. A cost names every part or says which parts it leaves out, gives its cap when it grows with use, and says who pays it in each period when it repeats, also after a grant or an offer ends. A period gives its start date. A date shows its weekday only when you checked it, and a date from a system says which time zone it uses. An unknown says who will get it, by when, and whether the reader should wait for it.
   - Every decision says who made it and when. When two facts conflict, say where each one came from, and give the action that is safe in both cases. In a comparison, when only one option lists an item, say whether the others include it.
   - At the first use, say who or what each person, company and tool is, and explain each word that has a special meaning in a tool or a team, such as a HubSpot "deal", unless every reader already knows it. Do the same for each abbreviation and version label, the title and the lines under it included. Explain a term in brackets or in a short sentence of its own, and never put two brackets in one sentence. Leave out a term that the reader does not need.
   - When something is not done or was skipped, say why in the same line, or say who will find out and by when.
   - An estimate of work gives realistic working hours and the same time in working days, one row per piece of work. It says who does the work and what the hours include and exclude. Never use an invented unit.
8. **Easy to scan**, except in plain text: in a list of different things, start each bullet with a bold label. For items that move from one state or tool to another, use a table: the item, where it is now or where a plan puts it, and where it goes, with the result in bold.
9. **Length:** a review, report, scope or plan has no more than 800 words of visible text, header and footer included. A post or email aims for 250 words and never goes over 350.
10. **What goes out:** save a review, report, scope or plan as one HTML file, and a post or email as a text file (Markdown when the email shows bold), unless I or a skill ask for another format. Put it in the folder I name, or next to files of the same kind, and give me its path on its own line, also when the Answer style makes the rest of your reply one line. Never publish it, for example as a claude.ai Artifact, unless I ask for that by name. No reference that the reader cannot open: no file path or step that works only on this machine, and no ticket or sprint numbers from a tracker that the reader does not use. When you update a page that I changed by hand, keep my changes.
11. **Check before you hand it over:** render an HTML page headless with a browser tool that is already installed, such as Playwright, and never install a package for this check. Count the words of its visible text. Confirm that there is no sideways scroll and that each text block fills the full width of its column, with no `max-width` on text. If you cannot render it, say that the check did not run. Count a post or email with `wc -w`. In the visible text, not the source, search for a `#` before a digit, for local paths, for a semicolon that joins two ideas, and for any word that is not explained. Last, read the skim layer alone, then the whole text, as its reader, cold. Fix each place where you would stop, guess or ask.
