# Reader-facing docs: a 2-minute skim

The global CLAUDE.md points here. Read this note whole before you write or change a text it covers, and apply the "Words" standard of the global CLAUDE.md throughout. Source: `AI/Reader-facing docs.md` in the Life-Engineering vault; `setup.sh --claude-md` installs it as `~/.claude/reader-facing-docs.md` on each machine. Edit the vault copy.

**Covers** every read-only text whose reader can be me or anyone else (a client, a manager, a team, a group chat): a review, report, scope, plan, team-chat post, email or app page. The reader skims it in 2 minutes at most and understands at once what is going on and, when it holds actions, what to do, with no unexpected confusion. **Never covers** your chat replies to me, what a script or wizard prints while it runs, code, commit messages, or a file that sets an AI agent's rules (a CLAUDE.md, a skill, Claude's memory). **Covers only with a trigger** a pull request or issue body, and a working document that its reader follows to do technical work (a spec, an engineering plan, a runbook, a setup guide, a handoff note).

**Triggers:** `2 minutes` (`2-minute`, `two minutes`, `2 mins`) or an all-caps Answer style word brings in each text the trigger is about that the request makes or changes, even a kind the first paragraph leaves out; a never-covered kind stays out. A kind brought in only by a trigger keeps its own file type and place, and the rules and parts of rules that name other kinds (3, 4, 5 and 9) do not apply to it. When the request adds to or changes part of a text, the rule covers that part; it covers the whole text when I ask for that, or when the trigger is about the whole text ("make sure the doc is a 2-minute skim", a new guide). `2 minutes` about a text this rule never covers (your reply, a skill) keeps its plain meaning: that text reads in 2 minutes at most.

**Precedence:** the Answer style shapes only your answers to me. For a text an all-caps word is about, it adds one thing: the text lists every step or item the request puts in it. That list wins over "not exhaustive" in Words, and goes on an attached page when it breaks the limit in rule 9. A how question without an all-caps word adds nothing to a text. A format that a skill, a tool or the project's own rules set ("Closes #12" in a pull request) wins; the rest of this rule still applies.

1. **Words** (the writing standard in the global CLAUDE.md) applies in full.
2. **The skim layer** tells the whole story alone: the title (what the text is), the opening sentences (the answer), the warning and "What to do" when the text has them, and the topic headings. Each heading gives its answer or its result in plain words ("BookIt costs $1,112 less in year one"); a state word such as "Done" may follow the answer, never replace it; a heading is never only a label, a section number or a count that says nothing ("2 quotes compared").
3. **A review, report, scope or plan**, in this order:
   - A title that says what this is. Under it, the date and the author (me, unless I name someone else).
   - One or two sentences: who asked, when, for what, and where things stand now. When the reader must choose, add the trade-off as plain differences and what you recommend, or that you recommend nothing and why.
   - The biggest risk, when there is one, as a warning. Its title says what happens, to whom and to how many. One paragraph gives the cause and whether the harm can happen today. "To stop this:" gives the fix, who does it, by when, and how it blocks the cause; a fix with no date says what keeps the risk away until then, or that nothing does.
   - "What to do", only when actions can start now: one item per action or decision, the owner's name first, then the due date. With no date, say so and say what waits on it.
   - One short section per topic. Its first sentences say what the thing is, whose it is, and where its numbers come from. Then the answer, then the detail.
   - Last, the sources in plain words, with the date on which you checked each live system.
4. **A team-chat post or email** has no title, date or sources line; an email's subject line gives the main result or the ask. It speaks to the reader as "you", in this order: one or two sentences on what this is and its main result (for a choice, your recommendation and the one difference that decides it; for news, what happened, its effect and its state now); the warning, when there is one, ending with the sender's next step; "Done:" with one line per item when you report finished work; then only the reader's actions under a heading with their count, steps as a numbered list and choices as numbered questions. When the reader acts for a group (a chair), first say what you need from them and by when. With more than one reader, each question names who answers. When a page is attached, a last line says what it holds. In plain text, a label ends with a colon.
5. **An app page whose job is to be read** (a report, a summary page): rules 1, 2, 6, 7 and 11 apply; forms, settings, navigation and the project's design system are out of scope. Its main action is a solid button that is easy to see. A prompt for text that an AI model writes at run time asks for short, plain sentences with just enough context.
6. **Decisions and actions.** Each item makes sense alone. A question gives the choices, what each one gains or costs, and the likely answer or what happens until the reader replies. A gain or cost is an amount in days, money or risk, never only "sooner" or "cheaper", and says how long it lasts; a gain that cannot be counted (goodwill) says what it brings, to whom, and why now. Write the question so that a one-word reply answers it and cannot mean two things ("After" or "Before"), without telling the reader to answer in one word; after each choice, one sentence says what that answer starts or changes, never a restatement of the question. List every choice the reader must make, also one that applies only after another. Test each action and the warning's fix against each choice: when one changes the other, say so in that choice. A task for later goes at the end of its topic section, not in "What to do": the owner first, then what starts it, then why it cannot start now.
7. **Just enough context**, so that the reader never has to ask:
   - Never a bare number or "#3": put the noun in the label ("Automations 3 and 11" under a column headed "Melbourne automation"); a sentence nearby is not enough. Before items that carry someone else's numbering, one sentence says whose numbering it is and why these items were picked.
   - Every number says where it came from and what it rests on, or that it is a guess. Count in one unit across the text, the one the reader acts on (people, not records); one currency and one time base, converted only at a stated rate; a figure you worked out shows its sum in words; a choice that weighs unlike things (money and volunteer hours) shows the rate at which they are equal. A proposed amount says who proposed it and why. A cost names every part or says which parts it leaves out, gives its cap when it grows with use, and says who pays it in each period when it repeats, also after a grant or an offer ends. A period gives its start date. A date shows its weekday only when you checked it, and a date from a system says which time zone it uses. An unknown says who will get it, by when, and whether the reader should wait for it.
   - Every decision says who made it and when. When two facts conflict, say where each one came from, and give the action that is safe in both cases. In a comparison, when only one option lists an item, say whether the others include it.
   - At the first use, say who or what each person, company and tool is, and explain each word with a special meaning in a tool or a team (a HubSpot "deal"), each abbreviation and each version label, the title and the lines under it included, unless every reader already knows it. Explain in brackets or in a short sentence of its own, never two brackets in one sentence. Leave out a term the reader does not need.
   - When something is not done or was skipped, say why in the same line, or say who will find out and by when.
   - An estimate of work gives realistic working hours and the same time in working days, one row per piece of work, who does the work, and what the hours include and exclude. Never an invented unit.
8. **Easy to scan**, except in plain text: in a list of different things, each bullet starts with a bold label. Items that move from one state or tool to another go in a table: the item, where it is now or where a plan puts it, and where it goes, with the result in bold.
9. **Length:** a review, report, scope or plan has at most 800 words of visible text, header and footer included. A post or email aims for 250 words and never goes over 350.
10. **What goes out:** a review, report, scope or plan is one HTML file, a post or email a text file (Markdown when the email shows bold), unless I or a skill ask for another format. It goes in the folder I name, or next to files of the same kind, and its path goes on its own line in your reply, also when the Answer style makes the rest one line. Never publish it, as a claude.ai Artifact or otherwise, unless I ask for that by name. No reference the reader cannot open: no file path or step that works only on this machine, no ticket or sprint number from a tracker the reader does not use. When you update a page I changed by hand, keep my changes.
11. **Check before you hand it over:** render an HTML page headless with a browser tool that is already installed (Playwright), and never install a package for this check. Count the words of its visible text. Confirm there is no sideways scroll and that each text block fills the full width of its column, with no `max-width` on text. If you cannot render it, say that the check did not run. Count a post or email with `wc -w`. In the visible text, not the source, search for a `#` before a digit, for a local path, for a semicolon that joins two ideas, and for any word that is not explained. Last, read the skim layer alone, then the whole text, as its reader, cold, and fix each place where you would stop, guess or ask.
