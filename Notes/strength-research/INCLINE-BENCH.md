## Findings

1. **Angle-specific 1RM in trained adults (best primary ratio source).** Rodríguez-Ridao et al. 2020 [#3] measured a separate 1RM at each inclination in 30 "young, healthy, and physically active adults" with ≥1 year of resistance training, ≥3 sessions/week (age 22.9 ± 3.0 y, body mass 76.7 ± 8.7 kg, height 1.8 m; sex breakdown not reported). Table 1: 1RM at 0° = 81.4 ± 15.5 kg; 15° = 72.0 ± 14.0; 30° = 63.3 ± 12.3; 45° = 57.9 ± 9.7; 60° = 52.2 ± 9.0 kg. Ratios incline ÷ flat: 15° 0.885; **30° 0.778**; 45° 0.711; 60° 0.641 (flat is 28.6% higher than 30°: 81.4/63.3 = 1.286). Load falls roughly 7–9 percentage points per 15° of inclination. 1RM protocol: 4–5 progressive sets per inclination (20 reps @~30%, 12 @~50%, 6 @~70%, 1 @~85%, then a maximal single "while maintaining an adequate technique").

2. **Competitive bench-press specialists (6RM, ±25°).** Saeterbakken et al. 2017 [#7], N = 12 national/international-level bench press athletes (age 34.3 ± 14.1 y, body mass 97.6 ± 18.3 kg, 1RMs 130–275 kg; wide 81 cm grip; "the barbell had to touch the chest gently without any bouncing", no pause command): 6RM flat 132.7 ± 17.1 kg, +25° incline 109.2 ± 11.1 kg, −25° decline 129.4 ± 13.7 kg. "The 6-RM load in the inclined bench condition … was 21.5% lower than in the flat condition and 18.5% lower than in the declined condition." Ratio incline ÷ flat = 109.2/132.7 = **0.823**. EMG: pectoralis/deltoid not significantly different across bench positions; triceps 58.5–62.6% lower and biceps 48.3–68.7% higher on the incline.

3. **Smith machine, sport-science students.** García-Ramos, Suzovic & Pérez-Castilla [#9], 24 students (12 men): 1RM horizontal bench press men 94.2 ± 14.6 kg vs inclined bench press 87.7 ± 11.6 kg (ratio 0.931); women 45.4 ± 10.1 vs 41.6 ± 6.6 (0.916). The angle is not stated in the abstract; Marques et al. [#10] describe this study as "inclined Smith machine bench press (45 degrees)". Individual 1RMs correlated r = 0.714–0.982 across exercises. (Guided bar and a non-specialist population; the ratio is higher than free-weight data.)

4. **Untrained men, free-weight, 45°.** Marques et al. 2025 [#10], 30 Saudi males with no resistance-training experience (26.4 ± 3.0 y, 72.6 ± 8.4 kg, 21.4% fat): 1RM horizontal 53.9 ± 10.5 kg vs 45° incline 52.5 ± 10.2 kg (ratio 0.974). Bar lowered 2–3 s to the chest, then concentric at maximal intended velocity; shoulder-width pronated grip; feet on floor. Together with #1–#3, the pattern is: the incline ÷ flat ratio is ~0.97 in untrained men, ~0.93 on a Smith machine in students, and **0.78–0.82 in trained/competitive lifters** (30° free weight and 25° 6RM respectively). The stronger and more bench-specialised the lifter, the bigger the flat-over-incline gap.

5. **Strength Level's implied ratio.** Interpolating the Strength Level male tables to 57 kg [#12, #14] gives incline ÷ flat = 0.853 (Beginner), 0.850 (Novice), 0.861 (Intermediate), 0.856 (Advanced), 0.859 (Elite); community averages 86 kg ÷ 96 kg = 0.896. These are all higher than the laboratory ratios in trained lifters (0.78–0.82), which is consistent with users logging inclines at heterogeneous (often 15–30°) angles, touch-and-go reps, and self-selection of people who incline-press a lot.

6. **Symmetric Strength hard-codes the ratio.** Its client-side code [#17] contains `if("Incline Bench Press"===a) return .82*e.percentOfPLTotal(r,t,s,"Bench Press",i)` and `if("Male"===t&&"Bench Press"===a) return .65*e.percentOfPLTotal(r,"Male",s,"Deadlift",i)` with the male deadlift share of a powerlifting total fixed at 0.396825. So its incline standard is always 0.82 × its bench standard = 0.2115 × an "expected" powerlifting total. This 0.82 constant matches the competitive-athlete 6RM ratio in #2.

7. **Why 30° is the usual recommendation (EMG evidence).**
   - Rodríguez-Ridao 2020 [#3] (0/15/30/45/60°, 60% of each angle's 1RM): "the highest EMG activity presented for the clavicular head was at the 30° bench press inclination"; middle and lower portions were highest at 0°; anterior deltoid highest at 60°; triceps similar at all angles. Upper-pec EMG at 45° was not different from 0°, so nothing is gained above 30°.
   - Lauver et al. 2016 [#1] (0°, 30°, 45°, −15°; 14 resistance-trained males, 21.4 ± 0.4 y; 65% 1RM): upper pec showed no difference over the whole concentric phase, but during the 26–50% contraction window 30° (122.5 ± 10.1% MVIC) and 45° (124 ± 9.1% MVIC) exceeded horizontal (98.2 ± 5.4%) and −15° (96.1 ± 5.5%); lower pec fell at 45° (71.9 ± 4.5% vs 100.1 ± 5.2% horizontal). Authors: horizontal bench activates both heads; 30°/45° adds upper-pec activation at certain time points.
   - Trebs et al. 2010 [#2] (0, 28, 44, 56°; 15 healthy males; 70% of each angle's own 1RM — i.e., angle-specific 1RMs were measured but the values are not in the abstract): clavicular head greater at 44° vs 0° (p = 0.010), 56° vs 0° (p = 0.013) and 44° vs 28° (p = 0.003); sternocostal head greatest at 0°; anterior deltoid greater at 28°, 44° and 56° than at 0°. Conclusion: do both flat and incline.
   - Coratella et al. 2020 [#8] (10 competitive bodybuilders; 45° incline; 80% of each exercise's own 1RM): clavicular head more excited on the 45° incline, sternocostal head less excited, triceps greater on flat/decline, anterior deltoid engaged in all except decline.
   - Barnett, Kippers & Turner 1995 [#6] (6 male weight trainers, four trunk inclinations × two grips at 80% of max): anterior deltoid activity "tended to increase as trunk inclination increased"; triceps long head more active on decline/flat and with a narrow grip; sternocostal head more active flat than decline. (Abstract via snippet only.)
   - Glass & Armstrong 1997 [#5] (15 weight-trained young men; +30° vs −15°, 6 reps): decline produced significantly greater lower-pectoral activation in both concentric and eccentric phases; no upper-pec advantage for 30° was reported. (Abstract via snippet only.)
   - Saeterbakken 2017 [#7] (25°): no pec/deltoid difference vs flat, but triceps sharply lower and biceps higher.
   Net: 30° is the smallest inclination at which the clavicular (upper) head is maximised (#3), before anterior-deltoid takeover (#2, #3, #6) and before load falls further (1RM at 30° ≈ 78% of flat vs 71% at 45° and 64% at 60°, #3). That is the mechanistic basis for choosing 30° as the test angle.

8. **Training-study note (Chaves 2020) [#4].** 47 untrained men (21.1 ± 3.3 y, 71.9 ± 13.5 kg, 176 ± 7 cm) trained once/week for 8 weeks with horizontal (n = 15), 44° incline (n = 15) or combined (n = 17) bench press. Outcomes were pectoralis thickness, EMG and **isometric** strength (kgf), not dynamic 1RM: "no difference between groups for the change in horizontal bench press isometric strength (~10 kg increase, p = 0.776) or incline bench press isometric strength (~11 kg increase, p = 0.333)". The brief's premise that this paper "reports 1RM for both" is not correct; it gives no incline ÷ flat 1RM ratio.

11. **Symmetric Strength incline standards for a 57 kg, 28-year-old male [#17] (computed from the site's own code, because the standards page is rendered client-side).** Strength score = Wilks-equivalent ÷ 4 with no age factor between 23 and 40 (`strengthScoreToWilks`: factor 1 unless age <23 or >40). Classification thresholds (`englishStrengthScore`): Subpar <30, Untrained ≥30, Novice ≥45, Intermediate ≥60, Proficient ≥75, Advanced ≥87.5, Exceptional ≥100, Elite ≥112.5, World class ≥125. Male Wilks coefficient at 57 kg (coefficients in `wilksCoefficient`: −216.0475144, 16.2606339, −0.002388645, −0.00113732, 7.01863e-06, −1.291e-08) = 0.8949. Standard = (4 × score ÷ 0.8949) × 0.396825 × 0.65 × 0.82. Results (metric, unrounded): Untrained 28.4 kg (0.50×BW); Novice 42.5 (0.75×); **Intermediate 56.7 (1.00×); Proficient 70.9 (1.24×); Advanced 82.7 (1.45×); Exceptional 94.5 (1.66×); Elite 106.4 (1.87×)**; World class 118.2 (2.07×). Corresponding flat bench: 34.6 / 51.9 / 69.2 / 86.5 / 100.9 / 115.3 / 129.7 / 144.1 kg. Interpretation from the site's About page: the score "measures how well you would fare in a strength competition against lifters at your sex and bodyweight"; ratios among lifts "were gathered from powerlifting world records, published strength standards, and other sources". It is a formula against strength athletes, not an empirical percentile of gym-goers.

12. **No federation contests the incline press.** The IPF Technical Rulebook 2026 [#18] defines only the flat bench press: the bench "shall conform to the following dimensions: Length — not less than 1.22 m and shall be flat and level. Width — not less than 29 cm and not exceeding 32 cm. Height — not less than 42 cm and not exceeding 45 cm". §4.2 "Bench Press" and §4.2.1 "Causes for disqualification of a bench press" contain no incline variant. The Europe PMC search [#30] and the site listings I could open show no governing-body incline records; the USAWA all-round rulebook (the one federation that sanctions many odd bench variants) could not be opened (HTTP 403/429) [#27], so "no federation" is verified for IPF and UNVERIFIED for USAWA/IAWA.

16. **Test definition (30° paused incline barbell bench press), adapted from IPF §4.2 [#18] plus the lab protocols [#3, #7, #10].** (a) Adjustable bench set to 30° measured from horizontal (a protractor/phone inclinometer on the backrest; most commercial benches' "30°" notch is close but check it). (b) Set-up: head, shoulders and buttocks in contact with the bench throughout; feet flat on the floor (blocks allowed); thumbs-around grip, forefingers inside 81 cm. (c) Start with elbows locked; lower under control (2–3 s) to the upper chest/clavicular line, hold the bar visibly motionless for ~1 s (no bounce, no heave, no sinking), then press to full lockout with no downward movement of the bar; racking after lockout. (d) Fails: buttocks or head lifting, heels lifting, uneven lockout, bar drifting down during the press, bounce. (e) Warm-up/attempt scheme per [#3]: 20 reps @ ~30%, 12 @ ~50%, 6 @ ~70%, 1 @ ~85%, then 2–3 max singles with 3–5 min rest. Converting a flat standard to incline: incline 1RM ≈ flat 1RM × 0.78 at 30° (trained lifters, [#3]); × 0.82 at 25° or as a rounded coaching constant ([#7], [#17]); × 0.71 at 45° and × 0.89 at 15° ([#3]). To convert a touch-and-go incline PR to a paused one, no incline-specific source was found (see Gaps); treat a paused rep as a stricter, slightly lower number.

## Caveats

- Strength Level data are self-reported calculator entries (no video, no angle, no pause standard); the site filters implausible/duplicate/automated entries but cannot verify form. Its incline ÷ flat ratio (0.85–0.86) exceeds every laboratory ratio in trained lifters (0.78–0.82), so a strict 30°, paused test will feel harder than the SL table implies; the flat-× -0.78 row is the conservative floor.
- Symmetric Strength is a Wilks-based formula with a hard-coded 0.82 incline constant, calibrated to "strength athletes" and world records, not a measured percentile; the site itself warns that even the strongest lifter in an average commercial gym may not reach "Advanced".
- Percentile interpolation inside the SL table (the bracketed 60–75th and 85–90th values) assumes load is linear in percentile; real distributions are skewed, so treat those brackets as ±3–4 kg.
- The lab ratios come from 30 trained adults at 76.7 kg (sex not reported) [#3] and 12 competitive benchers at 97.6 kg [#7]; none of the ratio studies used a ~57 kg population, a 14 cm wrist, or a competition pause. Shorter limbs (typical of small-framed lifters) shorten the bar path and favour pressing relative to bodyweight; that likely works in this subject's favour but is unquantified here.
- Chaves 2020 [#4] measured isometric strength, not 1RM, so it contributes no ratio; Chigira 2025 [#11] is retracted and was excluded; Trebs 2010 [#2] and Coratella 2020 [#8] measured angle-specific 1RMs but the values sit behind paywalls (UNVERIFIED).
- Cooper norms [#20] are from Cooper Clinic patients (general population, touch-and-go free-weight protocol) and are printed only at the 5/20/40/60/80/95th percentiles; the ACSM GETP 90th-percentile value (1.48) was not opened.
- Rep-max factors [#19, #20] are generic (NSCA) not incline-specific; individual 5RM-to-1RM ratios vary by several percent.
- The Huberman Lab guests (Galpin, Tsatsouline) give testing and programming rules but no incline or bench load standard; their numbers above are their stated opinions in the episodes, not population norms.
