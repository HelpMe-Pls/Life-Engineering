> [!note] Who this is for
> Male, 28 y, 170 cm, 57 kg (BMI 19.7), wrist 14 cm (height ÷ wrist = 12.1, a small frame on the usual index), self-described ectomorph. Trains barbell lifts, calisthenics and swimming. Question: what would  REALISTICALLY make him ==strong & athletic (above average strength and endurance) for his age and size== on incline bench, back squat, conventional deadlift, swimming (all four strokes), and bodyweight muscle-up and squat, all with immaculate form.

## 0. The grill (design tree, self-answered)

A grilling session needs live answers and this run was AFK, so each frontier question is listed with the assumption taken. Reply with a question number to change one and the table gets recomputed.

❓ **Q1 - Above average relative to whom?** General population, or people who actually train?
➡️ *Assumed:* **trained adult men** of the same bodyweight and age. Beating the sedentary population average is trivial (most adults cannot squat their bodyweight). General-population norms (ACSM / Cooper Institute) are shown alongside for context.

❓ **Q2 - Which percentile is "strong & athletic"?**
➡️ *Assumed:* three tiers. **Above average** = better than ~60–75 % of trained men (Strength Level "Intermediate", ACSM 70th–80th percentile, Cooper "Good"). **Strong & athletic** = better than ~85–95 % (Strength Level "Advanced", ACSM 90th percentile, Cooper "Excellent", military max-score or selection-competitive). **Elite** = top ~1–5 % (Strength Level "Elite", national-class amateur, USMS National Qualifying Time).

❓ **Q3 - How is "size" handled?** Bodyweight multiples, or bodyweight-specific norms?
➡️ *Assumed:* **bodyweight-specific norms at 57 kg** (interpolated between the 55 kg and 60 kg rows), reported in kg and as ×BW. Plain ×BW rules of thumb flatter light lifters because strength scales with roughly BW^0.67, so the ×BW targets here are deliberately higher than the "1.5× / 2× / 2.5×" folklore built around 80 kg men.

❓ **Q4 - Any age adjustment at 28?**
➡️ *Assumed:* **none.** Strength Level's age tables are flat from 25 to 40; masters coefficients start at 40; masters endurance swimming peaks at 35–40 and Ironman swim decline starts at 25–29, so nothing argues for a discount.

❓ **Q5 - What does "immaculate form" mean per test?**
➡️ *Assumed:* competition definitions. Squat and deadlift per IPF technical rules (hip crease below the top of the knee; locked knees and shoulders back, no hitching). Incline bench on a 30° bench with a visible pause on the upper chest, feet flat, hips down, full lockout. Muscle-up = strict bar muscle-up (no kip, no swing, full lockout). Bodyweight squat = hip crease below knee, about 1 s down and 1 s up, no rest at the top, unbroken. Swimming = legal strokes per World Aquatics SW 5–9, push start, 25 m pool.

❓ **Q6 - Which swimming metrics?**
➡️ *Assumed:* freestyle 100 m (speed), 400 m (aerobic), 1500 m and the Cooper 12-minute swim (endurance), the Navy 500 yd; 100 m of each other stroke; 100 IM and 200 IM for all four together.

❓ **Q7 - Bodyweight squat: max reps or timed?**
➡️ *Assumed:* both: unbroken reps to technical failure (primary) and reps in 60 s (secondary); pistol squats as the single-leg strength marker.

❓ **Q8 - Muscle-up: reps or "can you do one"?**
➡️ *Assumed:* max strict bar muscle-ups, with pull-up and dip prerequisites and the ring muscle-up as the athletic marker.

❓ **Q9 - Does "ectomorph" discount the targets?**
➡️ *Assumed:* **no.** Somatotype is descriptive, not a validated limiter. The 14 cm wrist is used only to classify frame size and to estimate a muscular-potential ceiling.

❓ **Q10 - Evidence hierarchy?**
➡️ *Assumed:* peer-reviewed > governing-body rules and time standards (IPF, World Aquatics, USMS, USA Swimming) > official military and certification standards > textbook norms (ACSM, NSCA) > large crowdsourced datasets (Strength Level, OpenPowerlifting; self-reported, trained population) > expert opinion (Huberman Lab guests) > blogs (never the source of a number).

## 4. Expert benchmarks from Huberman Lab guests (digest; full tables in [[EXPERT-BENCHMARKS]] and, for June 2024 onward, [[HUBERMAN-SWEEP]])

All are stated opinions on a podcast, not population norms, unless marked. Converted for 57 kg where relative.

- **Dr. Andy Galpin** (Cal State Fullerton kinesiology; six-part 2023 series and 2022 episode): grip 40 kg minimum, ideally past 60 kg; dead hang 30–50 s good, above 60 s "pretty happy" (light people hang longer, so treat as floors); broad jump ≥ your height (1.70 m); vertical jump ≥ 61 cm; leg extension 1×BW for one rep (57 kg) as his squat substitute; goblet hold ½ BW (28.5 kg) for 45 s; 25+ full push-ups; front plank 60 s, side plank 45 s; VO2max men above 50, "really good" at 55, endurance athletes 70–80; continuous non-walking work 20–30 min, ideally nasal breathing; bench 2×BW (114 kg) as the point where he stops testing the average person; verify a 1RM with 5 reps or fewer.
- **Dr. Peter Attia** (2022, 2023, July 2024): dead hang 2 min for a 40-year-old man (so at least that at 28); air-squat hold at 90° for 2 min; farmer carry ½ BW per hand (28.5 kg) for 2 min; VO2max at least the 75th percentile, target "elite" (top 2.5 %) for age and sex; broad jump 1.83 m as his own floor; the "deadlift BW × 10" line he later retracted as made up.
- **Pavel Tsatsouline** (Feb 2025): declines to impose standards, points to military PT tests; Zercher squat 2×BW (114 kg) as an athlete's goal; 20 strict pull-ups cited as the Marine Corps maximum; grease-the-groove at 75–85 % of 1RM, half the reps you could do, sets 10 min apart; strength work in the 1–6 rep range, threes and fours predominant.

## 6. Body context

What the frame, FFMI and "ectomorph" labels in §2 actually say, with the sources (main-session lookups, 2026-10-02; PubMed itself was rate-limiting, so abstracts came through Europe PMC).

**Frame size.** r = height ÷ wrist circumference = 170 ÷ 14 = ==12.1==. The index and its cut-offs (men: r > 10.4 small, 9.6–10.4 medium, < 9.6 large; women 11.0 / 10.1) are attributed by nutrition-assessment references and the calculators that reproduce them to Grant JP, *Handbook of Total Parenteral Nutrition* (Saunders, 1980). The primary text was not opened, so the attribution is second-hand. It is a clinical screening index for ideal-weight tables, not a strength predictor; its only job here is to feed the muscular-potential model below.

**FFMI.** Fat-free mass ÷ height²; Kouri, Pope, Katz & Oliva 1995 add 6.3 × (1.80 − height in m) to normalise to a 1.80 m man (*Clin J Sport Med* 5(4):223–8, PMID 7496846). At 57 kg and an assumed 12 % fat: FFM 50.2 kg → FFMI 17.4, normalised ==18.0==. Reference points: the median FFMI for men 18–34 in 2,986 Swiss men is ==18.9 kg/m²== (Schutz, Kyle & Pichard 2002, *Int J Obes* 26:953–960, doi 10.1038/sj.ijo.0802037; bioimpedance-based; the percentile table is behind the paywall, so only the median is cited); ESPEN's low-FFMI criterion for diagnosing malnutrition is < 17 kg/m² in men (Cederholm et al. 2015, *Clin Nutr*, PMID 25799486); in Kouri's sample the athletes who had not used steroids "extended up to a well-defined limit of 25.0", and twenty pre-steroid-era Mr. America winners (1939–1959) averaged 25.4. So 18.0 sits just under the population median for his age, one point above the clinical "low" line and seven points below the drug-free ceiling, which is what "untrained-average territory" in §2 means. At 65 kg and 12 % fat the normalised FFMI would be 20.4, above the median. Galpin's on-air ladder for men (*Perform* Ep. 2, 2024, raw FFMI) reads 17.9 / 19.1 / 20.4 / ≈22 / 23.5 at the 25th / 50th / 75th / 95th / 99th percentiles with 25 as the natural limit, which agrees with Schutz's median and Kouri's ceiling; on it his raw 17.4 falls below the 25th percentile, and the 75th percentile needs about 59 kg of lean mass, roughly 67 kg of bodyweight at 12 % fat ([[HUBERMAN-SWEEP]]).

**Muscular potential.** Casey Butt's model (weightrainer.net/potential.html; the book *Your Muscular Potential*, 2009) was fitted to drug-free bodybuilding champions and predicts maximum lean mass from height and bone girths: lean mass (lb) = H^1.5 × (√W ÷ 22.667 + √A ÷ 17.0104) × (1 + BF% ÷ 224), with H the height and W and A the wrist and ankle circumferences in inches. Recomputed in-session for 170 cm, a 14 cm wrist and an ankle of 20–22 cm: ==69.7–71.8 kg lean== at 10 % fat, i.e. a bodyweight of ==77–80 kg==. The page could not be opened on 2026-10-02 (broken certificate chain; the Wayback snapshot of 2022-05-24 was rate-limited), so the formula is reproduced from the model as published and is flagged for the verifier. Martin Berkhan's heuristic (leangains.com, "Maximum Muscular Potential of Drug-Free Athletes", 2010, updated) is "(height in centimeters − 100) = body weight in kilo ('shredded', i.e. 5–6 % body fat)", drawn from his natural competitors (169 cm → 69–71 kg, 181 → 79, 182 → 82, 186 → 86); for 170 cm that is 70 kg at 5–6 %, about ==75 kg at 12 %==. The two models agree within 3–5 kg, and the Butt ceiling converts to a normalised FFMI of 24.7–25.5, bracketing Kouri's limit of 25.0: three independent lines of evidence put the same ceiling on this frame. A ceiling after a decade-plus of training, not a target.

**Somatotype.** Sheldon's 1940 endomorph / mesomorph / ectomorph types survive as the Heath–Carter anthropometric somatotype (Carter & Heath 1990, *Somatotyping: Development and Applications*, Cambridge University Press): three continuous ratings computed from skinfolds, bone breadths, muscle girths and the height-to-weight ratio. It describes the physique you have today and moves as body composition changes; it is not a fixed genotype. What the trainability literature actually shows: in 36 physically active men, mesomorphy correlated positively and ectomorphy negatively with absolute 3RM bench and squat, mesomorphy alone explaining 31 % of bench variance (Ryan-Stewart, Faulkner & Jobson 2018, *PLoS One*, PMID 29787610), which is expected, since ectomorphs are lighter, and is exactly what the bodyweight-specific norms chosen in Q3 absorb; in 125 prepubescent children, eight weeks of training saw higher ectomorphy *raise* the odds of sprint (OR 6.5) and aerobic-fitness (OR 3.8) gains (Marta et al. 2013, *J Strength Cond Res*, PMID 24077372); in 41 young men, twelve weeks of individualised interval training produced a significant somatotype × training interaction, i.e. trainability differs by somatotype, with no direction given in the abstract (Chaouachi et al. 2005, *Br J Sports Med*, PMID 16306506). Net: ectomorphy predicts lower *absolute* loads, nothing about reaching a bodyweight-relative percentile, and nothing adverse about endurance. Q9 stands.
